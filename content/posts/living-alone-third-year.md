Three years ago today, I moved into this small forty-square-meter apartment. At the time I thought living alone was only a temporary phase. Instead, I stayed for three years and found myself less and less willing to leave.

## Year One: Fighting Loneliness

The first few months were the hardest.

After work, I would open the door to darkness and silence. No one was waiting for dinner. No one asked how my day had been. I could sit on the sofa all evening without saying a word, and no one would find it strange.

I tried many ways to push back against that loneliness:

- Keeping the television on as background noise
- Meeting friends for dinner every week
- Buying a houseplant, which later died

But those were only surface fixes. The real change came on a rainy night, when I suddenly realized that I felt lonely not because there was no one around me, but because I had never truly learned how to spend time with myself.

## Year Two: Building Order

In the second year, I began to take living alone seriously.

I built a simple rhythm for my life: wake up at seven, make coffee, sleep before eleven, and read for half an hour before bed. On weekends I cleaned the apartment and bought groceries for the week.

> The essence of living alone is learning to take full responsibility for your own life.

I started learning to cook. I began with the simplest tomato-and-egg dish, and eventually I could make a full table of food. The warmth of a working kitchen became the best medicine for loneliness.

I also returned to reading and writing. I used to say I never had time. Then I discovered that a night alone can contain more time than you know what to do with.

## Year Three: Enjoying Solitude

By the third year, I had fully adjusted to living alone. I had even begun to enjoy it.

I could spend a weekend afternoon doing nothing, just sitting by the window in the sun and listening to music. I could decide to watch a late-night film without checking with anyone. I could arrange the room exactly as I liked without worrying that someone else might find it odd.

Of course, living alone has inconvenient moments. When you are sick, even getting a glass of water is your own job. When you move, every box is yours to pack. But compared with the freedom it brings, those things feel small.

## In Closing

People ask whether I will live alone forever. I say I do not know. No one can predict the future, but for now, I enjoy this way of living.

The most important thing living alone has taught me is this: **happiness is not something you receive from others; it is something you learn to give yourself.** Once you know how to be with yourself, you can live well whether or not someone else is nearby.

If you are also living alone, I hope this brings you some comfort. Loneliness itself is not frightening. Being afraid of loneliness is. Try becoming your own friend. You may discover that you are more interesting company than you thought.
