In November, the air in Kyoto seems to carry the scent of red leaves.

I arrived early in the morning. When I stepped out of Kyoto Station, the sky was not fully bright, and only a few early risers moved through the streets. In the distance, a five-story pagoda appeared and disappeared in the morning mist like an ink painting.

## Morning Light at Kiyomizu-dera

My first stop was Kiyomizu-dera. To avoid the crowds, I reached the entrance at half past six. The leaves had not yet reached their peak, but they were already beautiful: deep red, orange-yellow, and pale green layered together.

Standing on the wooden stage and looking down, I watched Kyoto wake beneath my feet. Sunlight climbed slowly over the city and made the leaves glow.

> In Kyoto, time moves slowly enough that you can almost hear a leaf touch the ground.

## A Walk Along the Philosopher's Path

In the afternoon, I walked for a long time along the Philosopher's Path. The path follows a small stream, lined on both sides with cherry trees. There were no blossoms in autumn, but the red leaves reflected in the water had their own charm.

When I grew tired, I bought matcha ice cream from a small shop by the road. I sat on a stone beside the stream, eating slowly and watching the water pass, and felt myself grow quiet.

The walk felt unhurried in a way that is rare in ordinary life.

## Night Maples and Lantern Light

In the evening, I went to Tofuku-ji to see the maples at night. Light falling on red leaves felt completely different from daylight. By day they were bright and passionate; by night they became mysterious and graceful.

There were not many people, so it was easy to enjoy the scene quietly. An elderly couple walked side by side. The husband stopped now and then to take photos, and his wife smiled shyly, waving him off while her eyes were full of delight.

## Leaving

After five days in Kyoto, I was reluctant to leave.

Kyoto's beauty is not only in any single landmark, but in a quality that seems to settle into every corner: old, quiet, and composed. Here, you slow your pace without thinking, lower your voice, and begin noticing details that are easy to miss elsewhere: the texture of a leaf, the temperature of the wind, the taste of a bowl of soup.

Perhaps that is Kyoto's magic. It does not try to impress you. It simply waits for you to slow down enough to notice what is already there.
