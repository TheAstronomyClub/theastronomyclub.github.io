I first picked up *Walden* in college. After a few pages, I found it dull and put it down. Ten years later, reading it again felt like being struck awake. I wanted to underline every page and pause after every paragraph.

## Why Now

Perhaps life has simply been too noisy in recent years. Every day brings a flood of information, endless messages on my phone, meetings at work, and plans on weekends. I am busy, certainly, but late at night there is still a hollow feeling: what is all this busyness for?

It was in that state that I opened *Walden* again. Thoreau's words felt like clear water poured over my restlessness.

> "I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately..."

## Simplicity Is Not Poverty

Many people mistake Thoreau's simplicity for a kind of ascetic poverty. It is not. The "simplicity" he describes is about **removing what is unnecessary so time and energy can return to what matters**.

He built a small cabin by Walden Pond for 28 dollars, planted beans, and occasionally worked in town. He was not incapable of living more comfortably; he chose another way to live.

That made me reflect on my own life:

- So many clothes in the wardrobe, but only a few I wear often
- So many books bought, but how many actually finished
- So many apps on my phone, but how many opened each day
- So many acquaintances, but how many people I can really talk to

We own more and more things, while our own time and space become smaller and smaller.

## The Power of Solitude

Thoreau spent most of his time at Walden Pond alone. But he did not treat solitude as loneliness. He saw it as a necessary practice.

"I never found the companion that was so companionable as solitude." I read that sentence many times.

In an age that worships constant social contact, solitude can seem like a luxury, or even be mistaken for isolation. But Thoreau reminds us that solitude is not loneliness. It is a chance to speak with yourself. Only in solitude can you hear your inner voice clearly enough to understand what you truly want.

## After Finishing

The night I closed the book, I made a decision: starting the next day, I would keep one hour of solitude for myself every day. No phone, no messages, only what I wanted to do: read, write, walk, or do nothing at all.

A month later, I found that my anxiety had eased and my sleep had improved. More importantly, I began to feel the texture of life again: sunlight on my face, wind through leaves, the scent of hot tea. Things I used to ignore became clear again.

*Walden* may not change your life outright, but it can make you look at your life again. If your days feel too loud or too complicated, find a quiet afternoon and read it slowly.

You may, like me, pause at some paragraph and suddenly understand that life can be this simple, and perhaps should be.
